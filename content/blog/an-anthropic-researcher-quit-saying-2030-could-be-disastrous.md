Last week my feed was full of one story: a researcher at Anthropic resigned and warned that the AI labs are racing toward self-improving systems and gambling with our lives. The quote doing the rounds was that people building AI believe it could kill us all by the end of the decade.

My first reaction was the same as everyone’s. Then I got curious. What are the actual claims? Who is making them? And more practically, if even part of this is right, where does that leave people like us who write software for a living?

So I spent sometime reading the primary sources. This is what I found, and what I think it means. The facts are cited. The interpretation is mine, and I’m holding it loosely.

## 🧩 What actually happened

On 9 September, [Jacob Coxon](https://newsweek.com/anthropic-researcher-quits-warns-ai-could-kill-everyone-12418798), who spent three years doing pretraining research at OpenAI and then Anthropic, posted that he was resigning. His argument was not that AI is bad. It was that both labs are racing toward self-improving superintelligence, and that Anthropic in particular understands the risk but keeps going because it believes the alternative (someone less careful getting there first) is worse.

What made it more than one person’s opinion is that Evan Hubinger, Anthropic’s alignment science lead, publicly backed him and said he personally puts more than 10% on AI killing all humans within a decade. He also said there is no solved plan yet for aligning a superintelligent system.

That’s an insider, at the company with the strongest safety reputation, saying the number out loud. Worth reading, whatever you conclude.

## 📈 The three facts that matter

**1. How long AI can work on its own is doubling every few months.**

[METR](https://theaidigest.org/time-horizons) measures something simple: how long a task (in human working hours) can an AI agent complete at 50% reliability. From 2019 to 2024 that doubled every 7 months. Since 2024 it’s been closer to every 3 to 4 months. In their latest pilot with the major labs, the strongest agents handled tasks estimated at 16 to 20 hours of human work.

To be fair, METR itself warns the benchmark is saturating and estimates above 16 hours are unreliable. But the direction is not in dispute.

This is the number I’d watch, more than any “AGI by year X” prediction. Raw intelligence is one thing. An agent that can stay coherent for a full working day without supervision is what turns clever text into something that acts in the world.

**2. Entry-level knowledge work is already contracting.**

Anthropic’s CEO told [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/05/28/ai-jobs-white-collar-unemployment-anthropic) that AI could eliminate half of entry-level white-collar jobs within 1 to 5 years, naming finance, law, consulting and tech. Around the same time, SignalFire reported Big Tech new-grad hiring down about 50% from pre-pandemic levels.

**3. Even the experts don’t agree on the year.**

[Geoffrey Hinton](https://letsdatascience.com/news/geoffrey-hinton-calls-a-10-ai-extinction-risk-not-unreasonab-5498b795) told BBC Newsnight last week that a 10% chance of AI causing extinction within a decade is “not an unreasonable estimate”. Yann LeCun, equally credentialed, says current architectures cannot reach general intelligence at all.

Both are Turing Award winners. When people at that level disagree this widely, the honest conclusion is: nobody knows the year. Anyone who tells you the exact date is selling something.

## 💡 Intelligence is becoming cheap

Whatever you believe about 2030, one thing is already true. The cost of getting a reasonable answer to almost any question is approaching zero.

I used to assume that meant everyone gets smarter. Free intelligence, everyone can learn anything, the playing field levels out.

However, think about what Google did to facts. Before search, knowing things was a real advantage. After search, facts became free. But the gap between people didn’t close. Some people used Google to learn. Some used it to find articles that agreed with them. The ones who used it well pulled further ahead, and the ones who didn’t stayed where they were.

I suspect AI plays out the same way, just faster. Cheap intelligence is a multiplier. It multiplies whatever you bring to it. If you bring curiosity and a real problem, you get a lot. If you bring nothing, you multiply nothing.

## 🔑 If answers are free, where does the value go?

This is the question I actually care about, because it’s the one I can act on. My working answer is three places.

**Knowing which problem is worth solving.** The model will happily solve whatever you point it at. It won’t tell you that you’re pointing at the wrong thing. Problem selection was always the senior skill. Now it might be the main one.

**Telling a good answer from a wrong one.** When generating an answer costs nothing, checking it is where the cost moves. The model can’t grade itself for you. If you can’t tell when it’s confidently wrong, cheap intelligence is a liability, not an asset. Verification is the new literacy.

**Having the trust and permission to act.** Knowing what to do is worth nothing if you’re not the person allowed to do it. Someone has to sign off, be accountable, take the blame when it goes wrong. That’s not a soft skill. That’s a structural position, and AI doesn’t fill it.

A non-engineer version: it’s like having a brilliant intern who works for free and never sleeps. The intern is not your advantage. Your advantage is knowing what to hand them, being able to check their work, and being the person whose name goes on it.

## ⚙️ Routine work that needs no judgment goes first

This one I feel fairly confident about, because we’re already watching it in the hiring numbers. Work that’s mostly execution with a known pattern, where the judgment has already been made by someone else, is the first thing to get absorbed. That’s exactly what a lot of entry-level work is.

I don’t think that means entry-level people are doomed. I think it means the ladder changes shape. The old path was: do the routine work for a few years, absorb judgment by osmosis, get promoted. If the routine work goes, we need a different way to build judgment. How? Personally not sure yet.

## 👉 The bottleneck moved

If I had to compress everything into one line, it’s this: the bottleneck is moving from doing to deciding.

For most of our careers, execution was the expensive part. Building the thing took time, so the people who could build fast were valuable. That’s flipping. Execution is heading toward free. What’s getting expensive is direction: knowing what to build, why, and whether what came back is actually right.

“Work smarter” doesn’t capture it. It’s not that we need to be cleverer. It’s that the thing we’re paid for is changing.

## What I’m doing about it

Nothing dramatic. I’m trying to spend more of my time at the deciding end than the doing end. Specifying, reviewing, asking whether the problem is the right one. Letting the tools do more of the typing and being stricter about checking what comes back.

And I’m trying not to plan my life around a 2030 prediction that might not happen. If it doesn’t, this is still a reasonable way to work. That’s the test of a good plan: it shouldn’t need the prediction to be true.

Curious how others are reading this. What am I missing?

- METR: the length of tasks AI can do alone is doubling every 3 to 4 months. Agents now handle 16 to 20 hour tasks.
- Entry-level white-collar hiring is already down. Whether that’s permanent is disputed.
- Experts disagree on the year, not the direction. Nobody knows the date.
- Intelligence becoming cheap widens the gap, like Google did with facts.
